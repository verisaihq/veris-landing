The problem

Every answer you need is in a document written to keep you out.

A typical 10-K runs 180 to 250 pages. The business overview, which most investors reach, covers roughly 12 pages. Everything that moves stock prices — debt covenants, auditor qualifications, related-party disclosures, forward guidance caveats — sits in the sections retail investors almost never reach. The language is written by securities lawyers for regulators and institutional analysts who read filings as a full-time job.

The information is free. It has always been free. The SEC publishes every filing the moment it is submitted, accessible to anyone with an internet connection. The hours required to extract anything useful from 200 pages of legal text are what most investors do not have. So they act on incomplete information and absorb the cost without ever knowing why.

01
The document is public. The research gap is not.
Every 10-K filed since 1996 is publicly available on EDGAR at no cost. The format — dense, unstructured, written in regulatory language — functions as a practical barrier that keeps most retail investors out of the sections that matter.
02
Institutions pay analysts $300,000 a year to read what you are skipping.
Sell-side research desks employ analysts whose entire job is parsing quarterly filings. They are not smarter. They have more hours and better tools. That is the entire advantage, and it compounds every quarter.
03
A general AI tool will give you a confident wrong number.
General-purpose language models are not built for financial verification. They produce plausible answers without flagging uncertainty. In research contexts where a wrong figure carries real cost, an unverified answer is worse than no answer.
04
The 8-K dropped at 4pm. By 9:30am it was already priced in.
Filing alerts hit EDGAR after market close. Institutional desks read them overnight. By the time retail investors see the news the following morning, the investors who read the primary source have already acted on it.

What this costs you

The disclosure was on page 84. It moved the stock 60%. Most holders never read it.

A company's going concern warning was added to their 10-K during Q3. The auditor's note ran four paragraphs across pages 83 and 84, written in the qualified language that securities lawyers use when they are flagging serious risk without triggering immediate regulatory attention. Investors who read it sold. The stock dropped 60% over the following six weeks. The filing had been publicly available on EDGAR since the morning it was filed.

Academic research on retail investor returns consistently identifies the 4.3% annual underperformance gap against institutional portfolios. The gap does not come from inferior stock selection instincts. It comes from acting on incomplete information while institutions act on the primary source.

  • Debt covenant triggers, auditor qualification notes, and related-party loan disclosures appear in every major annual filing. They are among the highest-signal disclosures a company makes. They are also among the least-read sections by retail investors.
  • Institutional desks track these disclosures systematically, every quarter, across every holding. That is not an analytical advantage. It is a reading advantage, built on tooling that retail investors have never had access to.
  • The SEC publishes every filing the moment it is submitted. The data is free. The barrier has always been the hours required to extract anything from it.
